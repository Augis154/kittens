Core classes (shared, no rendering or sockets anywhere in here):

Vec2, Aabb — value types for math and bounds.
GameObject — abstract: id, position, size, alive flag, Update(dt).
Actor : GameObject — velocity, health, facing angle. Base for players and enemies.
Player : Actor — holds current Weapon, latest InputCommand, respawn timer.
Enemy : Actor — abstract; Rat and Mouse subclass it. The move decision is one virtual method — that method is the seam where behaviour objects go later.
Projectile : GameObject — owner id, damage, lifetime.
Pickup : GameObject — weapon, ammo, or health.
Weapon — abstract: fire rate, damage, spread, ammo. TryFire(shooter, aimDir) returns projectiles. Four subclasses to match your four gun sprites.
TileMap — grid of tile types, wall queries, spawn points. Load from a plain text file for now.
CollisionSystem — actor vs tiles, projectile vs actor, actor vs actor. A uniform grid is plenty.
SpawnDirector — decides what appears and when.
GameWorld — owns the entity registry and the tilemap. Tick(dt) runs one fixed step: apply inputs, move, collide, resolve damage, cull dead, spawn.

Transport DTOs — keep the protocol to four message types: Join, JoinAccepted(playerId, mapId), InputCommand(moveX, moveY, aimAngle, firing, sequence), Snapshot(tick, EntityState[]). EntityState is id, type, x, y, angle, hp, weapon id. JSON is fine at prototype scale; the DTOs are the boundary you'd swap to binary behind.

Server classes: GameServer (accepts connections), ClientConnection (one socket, inbound input queue, outbound snapshot queue), Match (a GameWorld plus the connection-to-Player mapping), ServerLoop (fixed timestep with an accumulator — 20 Hz is enough).

Client classes: GameClient (connection, latest snapshot), WorldView (interpolates between the last two snapshots so movement isn't 20 Hz chunky), InputHandler, Renderer, Camera, AssetManager, Screen with MainMenu / Lobby / InGame, and a render loop decoupled from the server tick.

That's roughly 25 classes — the right size for a prototype and a genuinely comfortable base to grow patterns into.

Two-week schedule, assuming you can run two tracks in parallel:

Days 1–2 are project skeleton, math types, tilemap loading from text, and a renderer that draws a map plus one sprite — while the other track strips the existing networking down to join-and-echo and proves two clients can connect. Days 3–4 close the loop: server-side movement, input round trip, two kitties visibly moving on both screens. Get here on time and the rest is downhill. Days 5–7 add wall collision, firing, projectiles, one enemy that walks at the nearest player, damage and death.

Week two: spawn waves and a second enemy type, then camera, HUD, four distinct weapons, pickups, then menu and lobby screens plus a hand-built level of a few rooms. Leave day fourteen empty. Networking always breaks on the last day.

One rule for these two weeks: no pattern refactoring. Just keep the core free of drawing and socket code and let the seams sit unfilled.

Where things will naturally want patterns later, briefly: enemy behaviour and weapon firing are the obvious swappable-behaviour slots; entity creation and network message dispatch want a registry; screens and player status (alive / downed / reloading / invulnerable) are state machines waiting to happen; projectiles are the reuse-pool candidate; sprites shared across dozens of entities are the shared-instance case; and your input is already command objects. Recognising the slots now is enough — don't fill them yet.

Suggestions for more depth:

Make the four kitty sprites four classes, not skins — a heavy tabby that's slow with more health and knockback resistance, a lean alley cat that's fast and fragile with a dodge roll. Nearly free, and it transforms how the game feels in co-op.

A darkness overlay with a soft light circle around each player is the single cheapest thing that makes a placeholder sewer tilemap look deliberate.

A downed-and-revive mechanic instead of instant death is what makes multiplayer actually mean something, rather than two people shooting in parallel. It's maybe forty lines.

Knockback on hit — rats get shoved backwards when shot. Trivial to implement, enormous feel payoff.

Vary enemies by role rather than by stat block: a swarming mouse (fast, weak, many), a chasing rat (medium), a spitter that keeps its distance, and later a fat boss rat. Three behaviours cover almost all the interest.

Between-room upgrade picks (choose 1 of 3: faster reload, extra projectile, tail whip) give you a natural modifier layer to build on later.

Things I'd deliberately cut from the prototype: procedural generation, client-side prediction, animation frames, and any persistence. All four are easy to add to a clean core and all four will eat your two weeks.
